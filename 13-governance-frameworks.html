NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)

Published January 2023. It's voluntary, sector-agnostic, and outcome-focused.

  • GOVERN (cross-cutting): risk culture, policies, roles, accountability, third-party risk, workforce diversity.
  • MAP: establish context (purpose, users, impacts), categorize the system, identify risks and benefits.
  • MEASURE: assess, analyze and track risks with quantitative and qualitative methods (testing, red teaming, metrics).
  • MANAGE: prioritize and treat risks, respond to incidents, monitor deployed systems, decommission.

It's supported by the AI RMF Playbook (suggested actions per subcategory) and Profiles (use-case-specific implementations).

Other NIST publications to name-drop

  • NIST AI 100-2 E2025: Adversarial Machine Learning taxonomy and terminology (evasion, poisoning, privacy, misuse, direct and indirect prompt injection). See module 01.
  • SP 800-218A (2024): Secure Software Development Framework (SSDF) community profile for generative AI and dual-use foundation models.
  • Dioptra: NIST's open-source testbed for evaluating AI model robustness against attacks.
  • Ongoing work on a Cyber AI Profile (CSF 2.0 applied to AI) and SP 800-53 control overlays for securing AI systems. Check the current status before citing details.

ISO/IEC 42001 and the ISO AI family

ISO/IEC 42001:2023: AI management system (AIMS)

  • The first certifiable AI management system standard. Think "ISO 27001 for AI".
  • Same high-level structure: clauses 4–10 (context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation, improvement), Plan-Do-Check-Act.
  • Requires an AI risk assessment, an AI system impact assessment, an AI policy, objectives, and a Statement of Applicability against Annex A controls (e.g. data for AI, AI system lifecycle, third-party relationships, information for interested parties).
  • Increasingly asked for in vendor due diligence.

Related standards

  • ISO/IEC 23894:2023: guidance on AI risk management (applies ISO 31000 to AI)
  • ISO/IEC 22989: AI concepts and terminology
  • ISO/IEC 5338: AI system lifecycle processes
  • ISO/IEC 42005: AI system impact assessment
  • ISO/IEC 42006: requirements for bodies certifying 42001
  • ISO/IEC 27001 / 27701: infosec and privacy management (integrate with 42001)
  • ISO/IEC 27090 (in development): security threats and controls for AI systems

EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)

The world's first comprehensive AI law. It entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in phases. It has extraterritorial reach: it applies to providers placing AI on the EU market and to deployers in the EU, wherever the provider is based.

Unacceptable risk: prohibitedSocial scoring · manipulative/exploitative techniques · untargeted facial-image scraping · emotion recognition at work/school · biometric categorization by sensitive traits · most real-time remote biometric ID in public for law enforcement · predictive policing based solely on profiling
High risk: strict obligationsAnnex III areas (biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services incl. credit scoring, law enforcement, migration, justice) + safety components of regulated products. Requirements: risk management, data governance, documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, robustness & cybersecurity (Art. 15), conformity assessment, registration, post-market monitoring
Limited risk: transparencyTell people they're talking to an AI (chatbots); label deepfakes; mark AI-generated content machine-readably
Minimal risk: no new obligationsSpam filters, AI in games, most business tools (voluntary codes)

General-purpose AI (GPAI) models

  • All GPAI providers: technical documentation, information for downstream providers, copyright policy, public summary of training content.
  • GPAI with systemic risk (presumed above 1025 FLOPs of training compute, or designated): model evaluations incl. adversarial testing, systemic-risk assessment and mitigation, serious incident reporting, adequate cybersecurity for the model and infrastructure.
  • The GPAI Code of Practice (July 2025) has chapters on transparency, copyright, and safety & security.

Article 15: the security article

High-risk AI must achieve appropriate accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, and be resilient to attempts to alter its use or performance by exploiting vulnerabilities. The text names AI-specific attacks: data poisoning, model poisoning, adversarial examples / model evasion, confidentiality attacks, and model flaws.

Good to quote: it shows the law explicitly expects AI-specific security controls.

DateWhat applies
1 Aug 2024Entry into force
2 Feb 2025Prohibited practices banned; AI literacy obligations
2 Aug 2025GPAI model obligations; governance (AI Office, national authorities); penalties
2 Aug 2026Most remaining provisions, incl. Annex III high-risk systems and transparency duties
2 Aug 2027High-risk AI embedded in regulated products (Annex I); legacy GPAI models comply

Penalties: up to €35M or 7% of global annual turnover for prohibited practices; up to €15M or 3% for most other obligations; up to €7.5M or 1% for supplying incorrect information. Watch: in Nov 2025 the European Commission proposed a "Digital Omnibus" that would postpone some high-risk deadlines. Check the current status before quoting high-risk dates.

Google Secure AI Framework (SAIF) and CoSAI

Google's SAIF (June 2023) has six core elements:

  1. Expand strong security foundations to the AI ecosystem
  2. Extend detection and response to bring AI into the organization's threat universe
  3. Automate defenses to keep pace with existing and new threats
  4. Harmonize platform-level controls for consistent security across the organization
  5. Adapt controls to adjust mitigations and create faster feedback loops (incl. red teaming)
  6. Contextualize AI system risks in surrounding business processes

Google also co-founded CoSAI (Coalition for Secure AI, OASIS, July 2024) with other major AI companies. Its workstreams cover AI software supply chain security, preparing defenders, and AI security risk governance, plus agentic security.

CSA MAESTRO: threat modeling for agentic AI

MAESTRO (Multi-Agent Environment, Security, Threat, Risk, and Outcome), from the Cloud Security Alliance (Feb 2025), is a layered threat-modeling approach for agentic systems. You analyze threats per layer and across layers.

L7 · Agent EcosystemMarketplaces, agent-to-agent interactions, users: impersonation, malicious agents, trust abuse
L6 · Security & ComplianceVertical layer across all: policy, compliance, security controls for agents
L5 · Evaluation & ObservabilityMonitoring, evals, logging: poisoned metrics, evasion of detection
L4 · Deployment & InfrastructureContainers, cloud, orchestration: compromised infra, lateral movement
L3 · Agent FrameworksLangChain, AutoGen, MCP: framework vulns, tool and supply chain attacks
L2 · Data OperationsRAG, vector DBs, pipelines: poisoning, exfiltration
L1 · Foundation ModelsThe LLMs: jailbreaks, backdoors, extraction

CSA also publishes the AI Controls Matrix (AICM) (2025), a vendor-agnostic control framework for cloud and AI, comparable to its Cloud Controls Matrix.

Government guidelines for secure AI development

UK NCSC + US CISA: Guidelines for Secure AI System Development (Nov 2023)

Endorsed by agencies from around 18 countries. Four lifecycle areas:

  1. Secure design: threat modeling, risk awareness, design trade-offs
  2. Secure development: supply chain, documentation, asset and technical-debt management
  3. Secure deployment: infrastructure protection, model protection, incident management, responsible release
  4. Secure operation & maintenance: monitoring, update management, information sharing

Others

  • NSA AISC et al.: "Deploying AI Systems Securely" (Apr 2024): hardening deployment environments
  • Joint "AI Data Security" guidance (May 2025): protecting data across the AI lifecycle
  • UK AI Cyber Security Code of Practice (Jan 2025): 13 principles, basis for an ETSI baseline standard (ETSI TS 104 223)
  • ENISA Framework for AI Cybersecurity Practices (FAICP)
  • US: federal policy shifted in 2025 (EO 14110 revoked; the July 2025 AI Action Plan emphasizes secure-by-design AI and AI-specific threat sharing)
  • India: no standalone AI law. The DPDP Act 2023 governs personal data (rules notified 2025), CERT-In handles incident reporting, and MeitY issued principle-based AI Governance Guidelines (2025)

Third-party / vendor AI risk questions

  • Is our data (prompts, files, outputs) used to train or improve models? Can we opt out contractually?
  • Retention periods for prompts, outputs and abuse-monitoring logs; data residency; sub-processors
  • Which models are used (and where they're from)? How are model updates communicated?
  • Tenant isolation for RAG, memory, caches and fine-tunes
  • Security testing: AI red teaming, pentests, bug bounty; can they share results?
  • Guardrails in place (injection, content safety, DLP); configurable by us?
  • Certifications: ISO 27001, ISO 42001, SOC 2; EU AI Act role (provider/deployer)
  • Agent/tool permissions: scopes, OAuth, admin controls, audit logs available to us
  • Incident notification timelines and AI-specific incident handling

AI incident response playbook

PhaseAI-specific actions
PrepareLogging of prompts, context sources, tool calls and model versions; kill switches; owner contacts; tabletop exercises with injection and leakage scenarios
Detect & triageWhat class is it: prompt injection, data leakage, poisoning, model misbehavior, abuse, cost attack? Which users, tenants and data are affected?
ContainDisable affected tools or agents; revoke agent tokens; quarantine poisoned documents and memory; tighten filters; roll back model or prompt version; block attacker accounts
EradicateRemove poisoned data from KB, memory, caches and embeddings; patch the vulnerable component; rotate exposed secrets
RecoverRe-enable gradually with monitoring; validate with red-team regression
Notify & learnRegulatory obligations (GDPR 72h breach notice; EU AI Act serious-incident reporting for high-risk/GPAI systemic risk; local rules such as CERT-In); add the attack to the regression suite; update the threat model
